Illustrative data feature

Who Gets the Cover?

Six seasons of runway bookings and magazine placements, sorted by skin-tone cohort, tell a story the industry doesn't put in a press release.

Every year, fashion trade press publishes diversity counts — how many models of color walked which runways. Those counts are real and useful, but they answer a narrower question than the one most readers actually have. Being cast is not the same as being seen: a model can walk a dozen shows a season and never land a cover, never anchor a campaign, never become the face anyone remembers. The gap between appearing and being centered is where colorism does its quietest work, and it rarely shows up in a simple headcount.

The scatter below is a composite, illustrative reconstruction — not a verified dataset — built to make that gap visible. Each of the sixty dots is a hypothetical working model from a recent fashion-week cycle, plotted by two independent measures: how often she walked (left to right) and how often she appeared on a magazine cover (bottom to top). Color marks a six-step skin-tone cohort, from fairest to richly deep. Scroll to see how exposure concentrates in the upper right — and how thin it gets everywhere else.

The fairest cohort owns the upper right.

Ten models make up this cohort in our sample. They cluster toward heavy bookings and frequent covers — the highest concentration of any group, out of proportion to their share of working models.

One shade darker, the picture already thins.

The fair cohort still books plenty of shows, but covers come less often. It's a small shift in the data and a large one in who gets remembered as the face of a season.

The middle of the scale is the middle of everything.

Medium-toned models sit near the center of both axes: enough bookings to stay working, rarely enough covers to become a name a casual reader would recognize.

For years, almost nothing here moved.

Diversity reports from this period show rising headcounts on the runway nearly every season — more faces, more agencies, more press releases about progress. But the cover data barely budged for the tan cohort across that same stretch: the same handful of names recirculated, the same clustering toward the lower half of the chart persisted year over year, and the "more diverse season" headlines kept arriving without a matching shift in who actually got centered. Casting expanded a great deal faster than editorial attention did, and the gap between the two is exactly what this chart is built to hold still long enough to see.

Deep-toned models book steadily, cover rarely.

This cohort is not underrepresented on runways in our sample — it's underrepresented in the rooms that decide who becomes a magazine's face. Bookings hold; covers thin further still.

Richly deep models show the widest gap of all.

The smallest cover share in the sample sits with the cohort closest to the runway's darkest end — the clearest illustration of why headcount alone was never the right measure of progress.

None of the figures above are drawn from a single verified dataset — they're an illustrative composite built to demonstrate how a season-by-season cover analysis might read, modeled loosely on the kind of reporting outlets like The Pudding have published on colorism in high fashion. The pattern it dramatizes, though, tracks a body of real industry reporting: runway diversity counts have climbed for a decade while editorial-cover diversity has moved far more slowly, and the lighter end of any given casting call still converts to cover time at a noticeably higher rate. Headcount answers "who was cast." It has never answered "who was seen."
