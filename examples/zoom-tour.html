How a Tree Learns to Split the Data

A decision tree over twenty-four invented homes, in the choreographic spirit of R2D3's 2015 essay A Visual Introduction to Machine Learning. One large canvas holds both the data and the tree it grows; the camera tours between them chapter by chapter while every regrouping and every split line you see is scrubbed live against your own scroll position — never a clock, never a separate timeline.

Zero author JavaScript beyond Moviola.init(). Scroll ↓ (or use ←/→)

Twenty-four homes, no labels yet

Picture every home a lender has ever seen scattered onto one chart, with elevation on one axis and square footage on the other. Nothing separates them yet — from here a home in San Francisco looks exactly like one in New York. A decision tree starts exactly this blind, with one pile of points and no idea which question to ask first.

The tree asks its first question

A decision tree learns by finding the single question that splits its data most cleanly, then repeating that trick on each half. Here the first question is elevation: draw one line through the cloud, and the homes above it already look less like the homes below it — the tree hasn't seen a single price tag yet.

Below the line: the flat homes

Every home the elevation split sent to the low side drifts together into its own corner of the canvas. They are not yet sorted by anything else — square footage, room count, walk score — but they have already been told apart from everything above that first line, using one threshold and nothing more.

Above the line: the hill homes

The other half of the split settles into its own region just as plainly. Two clusters now sit where one tangled cloud used to be, and neither the tree nor the reader has looked at a single price tag to get here — elevation alone did this much sorting, in one comparison per home.

The hill cluster asks a second question

One split rarely finishes the job. Inside the hill-home cluster, a second boundary appears — this time along square footage — because the homes above the elevation line still disagree along a feature the tree hasn't isolated yet. Watch the smaller half peel away as the new line draws in.

A leaf, finally pure

Zoom into the smaller half of that second split and every remaining home belongs to the same class. This is what a decision tree is chasing the whole way down: a leaf where no further question would change the answer, because there's only one answer left to give.

Not every branch ends this cleanly

The other half of the size split is not so tidy — a handful of homes here still disagree with their neighbors. A real tree would keep splitting this branch further; ours stops here on purpose, to show that purity isn't guaranteed after the first two questions, or even the first five.

The tree that grew while you scrolled

Every split you just watched happen inside the data has a matching branch here: elevation first, then size, with one pure leaf at the end of the shortest path. A trained decision tree is nothing more than this shape, stored as thresholds instead of drawn as lines on a chart.

Same twenty-four homes, fully sorted

Pull back and both views sit side by side — a data space carved into regions, and the tree of yes/no questions that carved it. Neither one is a real city; the choreography is the same recursive idea real classifiers use, run here on twenty-four invented houses so every step of it can be watched.

Nine camera flights (data-camera/data-focus/ data-zoom) tour one large SVG stage in a single continuous sweep; the two home clusters and both split bars move continuously under data-scrub, keyed to the very same chapters the camera is flying through — never a separate clock.
