How a classifier learns

Scroll to watch a straight line find the boundary between two classes — one continuous adjustment at a time.

Start with a cloud of points.

Before it learns anything, a classifier just sees points scattered in space — no labels, no structure, only their coordinates. Feed it enough examples like these, though, and a pattern usually starts to emerge on its own, waiting for a boundary to reveal it.

Label them, and take a first guess.

Give each point a label — circle or diamond, spam or not spam — and the model draws its very first boundary: a straight guess, planted almost at random. It will be wrong for plenty of points, and that is exactly how training begins.

Every mistake is a signal.

Every point on the wrong side of that line contributes an error, and the model measures precisely how far off each miss is. Those errors become a signal: nudge the line a little, in the direction that would have made more of them correct.

The margin starts to widen.

Nudge after nudge, the boundary rotates toward a cleaner split, and the gap between the two classes — the margin — starts to widen. A wider margin means the model is not just separating today's points, but leaving room for ones it has never seen.

The updates shrink to almost nothing.

Eventually the updates shrink to almost nothing: the boundary has settled where moving it further would only make things worse. This resting point, not any single dramatic leap, is what training actually optimizes for.

And it works on data it has never seen.

Hand the finished boundary a point it never trained on, and it still knows which side to put it on. That is the entire point of learning a rule instead of memorizing examples — one line, one continuous adjustment at a time, that generalizes.
